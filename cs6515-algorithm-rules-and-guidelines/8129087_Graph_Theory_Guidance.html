Graph Theory Guidance

Staff Guidance Graph Theory Reductions & Black Boxes
Reduction methodology, strict black-box invariance, and adjacency list standards

Below you will find official information on how you should approach solving Graph Theory problems in CS6515. Feel free to ask questions about writing a Graph Theory solution in this thread.

Context: The Reduction Philosophy

A graph theory problem in this course is fundamentally an exercise in reduction. You are given a computational problem, and your objective is to reduce it to a graph formulation such that a known, unmodified course algorithm solves it for you.

Strict Black-Box Rule: Known course algorithms (Post #192) must be treated as atomic black boxes. You are not allowed to modify or alter their internal execution in any way. Re-implementing a known algorithm with minor modifications is explicitly prohibited and penalized.

Instead of changing the algorithm, you modify the problem input or construct an auxiliary graph $G' = (V', E')$ representing the problem state. This graph is passed into the black box. You then extract and post-process the black-box output to form your final answer.

Mandatory Rules

  • NO PSEUDOCODE: Solutions written as pseudocode will suffer substantial grade deductions. Write in conceptual English prose.
  • Whitelist Only: You may only use approved black-box algorithms from the course whitelist.
  • Black Boxes are Invariant: Never modify a black box algorithm internally.
  • Adjacency List Model: All graphs are assumed to use standard adjacency list representations.
  • Variables $n$ and $m$: Assume you always know $n = |V|$ (vertex count) and $m = |E|$ (edge count) for runtime calculations.

Required Parts of a Graph Theory Solution (The 3-Part Rubric)

(a) Algorithm

  • Describe "how" to solve the problem in clear English words (narrative / paragraph form).
  • Bulleted conceptual steps are fine; line-by-line pseudocode is prohibited.
  • Describe what modifications or constructions are performed to the input (e.g. vertices $V'$, edges $E'$, capacities, or edge weights).
  • Explicitly name the black-box algorithm(s) invoked.
  • State what parameters are passed in as input to the black box.
  • State what data is extracted from its output.
  • Describe any post-processing performed on the output.
  • State the final return value.

(b) Justification of Correctness

  • Describe "why" your algorithm solves the problem in words (narrative).
  • Informal conceptual justification is expected; a formal inductive proof is not required.
  • Explain why your graph construction captures the constraints of the original problem.
  • Explain how the black box guarantees the desired property (e.g. shortest path, max flow, MST cut optimality) on your constructed graph.

(c) Runtime Analysis

  • Analyze all steps of your algorithm in worst-case Big-O notation.
  • Assert standard black-box runtimes without re-proving them (e.g. Dijkstra with min-heap is $O((n + m) \log n)$).
  • Analyze the runtime cost of all graph pre-processing and post-processing steps.
  • Provide an overall final runtime in fully simplified Big-O notation.

  • Tightest Worst-Case Bound: While we work with worst-case runtimes, we also require the tightest bound. For example, even though an $O(n + m)$ traversal reaches $O(n^2)$ when $m = O(n^2)$, it is incorrect to write $O(n^2)$ unless the graph is explicitly known to be dense or a clique. $O(n + m)$ is the correct, tightest worst-case bound.

  • If the graph is known to be a tree, $m = n - 1$, so $O(n + m)$ collapses to $O(n)$.

Graph Representation & Common Primitives

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all graphs in CS6515:

  • Are simple graphs (no multi-edges and no self-loops).
  • Are stored in adjacency list format: an array of $n$ vertices where each element points to a singly-linked list of outgoing/incident edges.
  • Vertices can be looked up by array index in $O(1)$ time.
  • May be connected or disconnected, directed or undirected, sparse or dense (assume the general case unless specified).
  • MST problems assume connected, undirected graphs with weighted edges.
  • Max Flow problems assume connected, directed graphs with capacities $c(u, v) \ge 0$.

Standard Primitives (All $O(n + m)$ Cost)

The following operations are standard and can be stated directly without low-level implementation details:

  • Reversing a graph: "Reverse the edges of $G$ to form $G^{rev}$" ($O(n + m)$).
  • Copying a graph: "Create a copy of $G$ called $G_{copy}$" ($O(n + m)$).
  • Filtering subgraphs: "Create a subgraph $G'$ retaining only edges with even weight" or "retaining vertices with degree $> k$" ($O(n + m)$).
  • Constructing residual graphs: "Create residual graph $G_f$ using the flows from Edmonds-Karp" ($O(n + m)$).

Discussion Key Takeaways & Staff Clarifications

7 discussion threads • Staff rulings
  • Multiple Black Boxes & Custom Traversals: Chaining multiple black-box algorithms or inserting an $O(n + m)$ custom BFS/DFS scan between black-box invocations is completely valid, as long as each black box itself remains unmodified.
  • Direct Input Graph Modification: You may modify the input graph directly in pre-processing (or construct a new graph $G'$); just account for the $O(n + m)$ transformation cost.
  • Adjacency List Size in Space Analysis: An undirected edge $\{u, v\}$ appears twice (in $u$'s list and $v$'s list), requiring $2m$ linked list nodes. In Big-O notation, constant factors drop, yielding $O(n + m)$ total space.
  • Exam Proofs vs Lecture Proofs: Lectures emphasize proofs of the black boxes themselves (e.g. Cut Property, Dijkstra correctness). On exams, you do NOT re-prove the black box; your correctness argument focuses entirely on justifying why your graph reduction maps the original problem correctly into the black box.
  • Tree Simplification: In trees, $m = n - 1$. An $O(n + m)$ traversal collapses to $O(n)$. When reporting black-box complexities (e.g. BFS is $O(n + m)$), simplifying to $O(n)$ on trees is valid but not strictly mandatory.

      When it is a tree, we know that we have n vertices and exactly n-1 edges.

      If the input you are given is a tree, then O(n) and O(n + m) are the same, given that m has an exact definition, which enables simplification to O(n).

      The adjacency list, like our normal lists, do not store any metadata. So, no shortcuts on length finding.

      You'll see through the way the book and lectures talk, and when we get to the programming homework, that "metadata" such as lengths, weights, colors are actually separate structures entirely. The graph theory black boxes post in #192 also treats them this way.

      Given that, if you need to find the vertex with the largest degree, you'll need to count the number of neighbors for every vertex in the adjacency list, and you'd end up at O(n + m) overall.
